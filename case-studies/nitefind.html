UX RESEARCH·PRODUCT DESIGN·CONCEPT

NiteFind

Nightlife discovery without the decision fatigue.

Mobile product · 10 weeks · Sole designer

WHY
01 / why this exists

Choosing where to go out shouldn’t take longer than actually going out.

People already have plenty of ways to find bars, events, and venues. The problem I found wasn’t access to options. It was piecing together enough information to trust one.

Interviews showed people bouncing between platforms to check atmosphere, crowd level, price, photos, events, and whether a place was actually worth going to. NiteFind became an exploration of how to bring those signals together without creating another overwhelming discovery app.

4 interviews→recurring needs→1 product direction
SIGNAL
02 / the signal

The interviews pointed to three recurring needs that mattered more than demographics: speed, confidence, and discovery.

01 speed

What’s happening right now?

Spontaneous plans made stale information especially costly. People wanted current crowd levels, events, specials, and nearby options without checking several sources.

02 confidence

Will I actually like this place?

Photos weren’t enough. Vibe, crowd size, price, noise level, and atmosphere helped people decide whether a venue was worth committing to.

03 discovery

Show me something that fits, not another top-ten list.

Participants described places through qualities like chill, chaotic, romantic, queer-friendly, hidden gem, photo-worthy, and speakeasy rather than conventional venue categories.

the insight

Different behaviors pointed to the same underlying need: reduce the number of decisions between “I want to go out” and “I’m somewhere good.”

DECISIONS
03 / key decisions
01

The research narrowed the product before I designed it.

Three recurring needs became three product priorities.

01research signal

Vibe before category

People described the night they wanted more naturally through atmosphere than venue type.

→product response

Filters centered on qualities like romantic, chill, live music, crowd level, price, and niche tags rather than relying only on traditional venue categories.

02research signal

What’s happening now

A good venue wasn’t useful if the information about it was stale.

→product response

Crowd indicators, current events, specials, distance, travel time, and venue activity became first-class information.

03research signal

Reduce the choice

Sometimes people didn’t want better search tools. They wanted help making the decision.

→product response

Quick Pick surfaced a small set of recommendations matched to location and preferences rather than returning another long list.

from requirements to structure

Structure before polish.

With the core signals defined, I mapped the product around three high-value moments: discovering somewhere nearby, evaluating whether it fit the night, and getting to a decision quickly.

02

Midnight Grid over Alt Scene

Alt Scene captured nightlife more literally: eclectic, expressive, and energetic. I liked it more in isolation.

But NiteFind was supposed to reduce cognitive load. Adding another loud visual layer worked against that goal.

Midnight Grid won because the interface needed to organize nightlife, not compete with it.

03

Three tests. Four changes worth making.

3 participants · in person · mobile prototype

observationdesign response
People hesitated to share location with no context.
→
Added a plain-language explanation and a way to continue without sharing location.
Quick Pick didn’t clearly explain why a venue was being recommended.
→
Added visible preference and filter context so the recommendation felt less arbitrary.
Saving a venue lacked clear feedback.
→
Added confirmation and a dedicated destination for saved venues.
Advanced filters weren’t specific enough.
→
Expanded the vocabulary toward more descriptive and niche qualities.

the fix, in the product

PRODUCT
04 / the product

Less searching. More deciding.

The final product organizes discovery around the information people said they needed before committing to a place.

find

Start with what’s nearby.

Location, crowd level, price, vibe, and distance make the map useful before opening a venue.

filter

Describe the night, not the category.

Vibe and niche filters let people narrow options using the qualities that surfaced during research.

evaluate

Put decision-making information up front.

Atmosphere, crowd level, pricing, travel time, events, specials, and imagery answer questions people were already checking elsewhere.

decide

When choosing is the problem, reduce the choices.

Quick Pick turns preferences and location into a short recommendation set instead of another long results list.

save

Keep “maybe later” easy to recover.

RESPONSIVE
05 / responsive design

Same decision hierarchy, more room.

Larger breakpoints created room for imagery, events, and supporting information without changing what mattered first. The hierarchy stayed centered on answering the same question: is this somewhere I want to go?

REFLECTION
06 / looking back

What I’d do differently now.

NiteFind taught me that research doesn’t need to produce more artifacts. It needs to make the next decision clearer.

If I continued the project, I’d recruit a broader participant group and test the core recommendation model more deeply, particularly how much people trust crowd data and why Quick Pick recommends a venue.

I’d also move into rough sketches sooner. I spent too much time refining early layout directions before proving the underlying structure.

The biggest takeaway wasn’t a feature or screen. It was learning to ask whether every design decision reduces uncertainty or adds another thing for the user to process.
